01 — What it's for

They're asking three things

Every question in every interview, however it's dressed up, is one of three questions underneath. Once you can hear which one is being asked, you know what your answer has to prove.

1

Can you do the job?

Skills, tools, evidence. Your portfolio and your projects. "Talk me through…", "How would you…", "What tools do you use…"

2

Will you fit the team?

How you work with people, handle feedback and cope when things break. "Tell me about a time…" questions live here.

3

Do you actually want this?

Motivation and homework. "Why us?", "Why this role?", "What do you know about what we make?"

What most candidates get wrong

They answer the question they wish they'd been asked

Asked about teamwork, they talk about their favourite game. Listen to the question, then answer that question first.

They claim instead of prove

"I'm a good team player" is a claim. "On Low Tide, I merged everyone's levels every Friday so nobody lost a week's work" is proof. Panels score proof.

They haven't looked at the company

Not knowing what the studio makes is the fastest way to lose an interview. Ten minutes on their website before you go in is enough to avoid it.

They have no questions at the end

"No, I think you've covered everything" sounds like you're not that interested. Always bring two questions.

An interview goes both ways

You're also finding out whether you want to work there. That's not just a nice thought — remembering it makes you calmer, and calm candidates come across better.

02 — Prepare

Preparation is most of it

You can't predict every question, but you can walk in with your research done, five stories ready and two questions to ask. Tick these off as you go — your ticks save in this browser.

The STAR method

For any "tell me about a time…" question. It stops you rambling and makes sure you finish on the bit that matters: what happened because of what you did.

S

Situation

Where and when. One sentence.

T

Task

What needed to happen, and your part in it.

A

Action

What you did. "I", not "we". The longest part.

R

Result

What changed, and what you learned.

Without STAR

"Yeah, we had a group project and there were some problems with people not doing their bit, but we sorted it out in the end and it was fine."

With STAR

S In our first-year group game, two weeks before the deadline, we had three different versions of the level and none of them worked together. T I was responsible for the build. A I set up one shared project, gave everyone their own sub-level, and merged them every Friday in a ten-minute meeting. R We handed in on time with one working build, and I now set up shared projects on day one.

Build your five stories

Five real situations will cover nearly every behavioural question. Write a line for each part. You can reuse the same story for different questions.

03 — What to wear

One step above the team

Most games studios are casual — hoodies and trainers on a normal day. That doesn't mean you turn up to the interview in one. The rule is simple: dress one step smarter than the people who work there. Nobody has ever lost a job for being slightly too smart; plenty have lost one for looking like they didn't care.

Games studio · creative agency

Smart casual

  • Clean, plain top or shirt, no slogans
  • Dark jeans or chinos with no rips
  • Clean shoes or plain trainers
  • An optional jumper or overshirt
College · university · larger company

Smart

  • Shirt or blouse
  • Trousers or a skirt, not jeans
  • Proper shoes
  • A jacket if you have one; a tie is not usually needed
Not sure?

Go smarter

Look at the studio's website or LinkedIn photos to see what the team wears. If you still can't tell, go smart. You can always take a jacket off.

The bits people forget

Clean beats expensive

Nobody expects new clothes. They do notice creases, stains and a strong smell of last night's chippy. Sort it the night before.

Be comfortable

Wear something you've worn before. A brand-new shirt that's too tight or shoes that hurt will make you fidget.

Take the hat off

Caps, beanies and headphones round your neck all come off before you go in. Phone on silent and in your bag, not on the table.

Show who you are — a little

A plain top with a small, subtle detail is fine at a games studio. A t-shirt from their own game is a risk. It might charm them or look like you're trying too hard.

Online interviews

Dress the same

At least from the waist up, and do the whole outfit so you don't forget if you have to stand up. Test your camera the day before.

Set the scene

Light in front of you, not behind. Camera at eye level. A plain or tidy background. Headphones with a mic. Close everything else and turn off notifications.

05 — Practise

Say it out loud

Answers that sound good in your head fall apart when you say them. Pick a question at random, answer it out loud to a partner (or your phone's recorder) within the time limit, then swap. Your partner listens for the three things underneath.

The listener's job

Did they answer the question?

The actual question, in the first sentence or two — not a warm-up story.

Was there proof?

A named project, a tool, a number, a result. Count them.

Did it end well?

On a result or what they learned, not trailing off with "…so yeah."

06 — On the day

From the door to the handshake

  1. Arrive ten minutes early Not thirty — that makes them feel rushed. Know where you're going, and leave time for buses being buses.
  2. Be polite to everyone The person on reception may be asked what you were like. Often they are.
  3. The first minute Stand when they come to meet you, smile, say hello and use their name if you know it. Sit when you're invited to.
  4. While answering Sit back in the chair with your feet flat and your hands still on the table or your lap. Look at the person who asked, and glance at the rest of the panel too.
  5. Take a breath before you answer A two-second pause looks thoughtful, not stuck. "That's a good question — let me think for a second" is completely fine.
  6. If you don't know Say so, then say how you'd find out. "I haven't used Perforce, but I've used Git every day, and I'd expect the ideas to carry over" is better than guessing.
  7. Your questions Ask the two you prepared. If they've already been answered, say so and ask a follow-up.
  8. Leave well Thank them, say you'd like the job if you would, and ask what happens next. A short thank-you email the same day is a good touch.

Nerves

Everyone has them

Panels expect it and allow for it. Shaky hands for the first minute won't count against you. What counts is whether you recover and answer.

What helps

  • Practise out loud the day before (section 05)
  • Breathe in for four seconds and out for six before you go in
  • Bring water
  • Have your notes in your bag. You can look at them before you go in.

Questions worth asking them

  • "What would a good first three months look like for someone in this role?"
  • "How does the team give feedback on work?"
  • "What's the project the team is most excited about right now?"
  • "What do the best people in this role do differently?"
  • "Is there anything in my application you'd like me to say more about?"
  • "What happens next, and when should I expect to hear?"

Avoid asking first about…

Pay, holidays or "how soon can I work from home". Those are fair questions, but they belong once an offer is on the table, not in your first interview.

07 — Your interview

The role you're applying for

This is the job you'll be interviewed for. It isn't real, but the panel will treat it as if it were: a three-person panel, real questions, and scored feedback afterwards. Read the advert the way you'd read a real one, and prepare against it.

How it runs

2 minWelcome and introductions
10 minSix questions from the panel
2 minYour questions for them
1 minClose
5 minPanel scores, then feedback to you

Bring

  • Your CV, printed (you're expected to know it)
  • Your portfolio or website on a laptop or phone, ready to show one piece
  • Two questions for the panel, written down
  • Your five STAR stories, for reading before you go in

You'll be scored on

  • Preparation and knowledge of the role
  • Evidence of skill (your work)
  • Teamwork and handling feedback
  • Motivation
  • Communication: clear, full answers in the right register
  • Professional presence: arrival, dress, manner

Why this counts

The recorded mock interview is evidence for AC5 Communication. Arriving prepared, on time and dressed for it is evidence for AC4 Professional practice. Write it up in your reflective log the same day: what went well, what the panel said, and what you'd do differently. Never write what without why.
