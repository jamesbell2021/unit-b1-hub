01 — Three things

Same name, three jobs

"Personal statement" gets used for two different documents, and "cover letter" gets confused with both. They are not interchangeable. Sending the wrong one — or writing one as if it were another — is one of the most common mistakes in an application, and it is entirely avoidable.

For a university or college course

UCAS personal statement

Three answers in three boxes. At least 350 characters each, 4,000 in total. About the subject — why you want to study it and what has prepared you.

One statement goes to all five of your choices, so it cannot name a university.

At the top of your CV

CV profile

Three or four lines. Who you are and what you do, compressed. Sometimes also called a personal statement, which is where the confusion starts.

Says what you do within the first eight words. Tailored lightly for each application.

With a job or role application

Cover letter

One page. Addressed to a person. Why this role, at this studio, and why you. Rewritten from scratch for every application.

The only one of the three that names who you're writing to and asks for something.

02 — UCAS statement

Three questions, not an essay

Since 2026 entry, UCAS no longer asks for one free-flowing essay. You answer three separate questions in three separate boxes. Older advice online — including from teachers who applied years ago — will tell you to write a single statement with a strong opening hook. That advice is now out of date.

3

questions

Each in its own box. Answer the question that is asked, not the one you wish had been asked.

350

characters minimum

Per answer. Characters, not words — that's roughly 55–60 words. A short answer simply will not be accepted.

4,000

characters total

Across all three. Spaces count. UCAS shows a live counter for each box and for the whole thing.

What each question is really asking

  1. Why do you want to study this subject or course? The moment it clicked, and what you've done about it since. Show you've looked at what the course actually teaches — the modules, not the title. Connect it to where you're heading.
  2. How have your qualifications and studies prepared you? Skills, not grades. Your grades are already on the form. What did your BTEC projects actually teach you to do — scoping, teamwork, debugging, iterating on feedback? Name the project.
  3. What experiences outside education have prepared you, and how? Jobs, jams, communities, caring responsibilities, things you built for yourself. The how is the part that counts — what you learned, not a list of what you did.

Because it goes to five places, it cannot name any of them

Every one of your choices sees the same three answers. Write "I want to study at Ulster because…" and the other four read that too. Write about the subject instead, and make sure all five courses are close enough that one set of answers fits them all.

Phrases admissions tutors are tired of

Cut these

  • "Ever since I was young…"
  • "I have always had a passion for…"
  • "From a young age I have been fascinated…"
  • "This world-class university…"
  • Any quote from someone famous

Replace them with

One specific moment you could actually describe — a project, a problem, a build that went wrong. Anyone can claim a passion. Only you can describe the afternoon you rebuilt a jump mechanic three times because the first two felt floaty.

03 — Cover letter

One page, one person

A cover letter is the opposite of a UCAS statement. It goes to one organisation, it names them, it names the person reading it, and it asks for something. If it could be sent unchanged to another studio, it isn't finished.

The shape of it

  1. Address a real person "Dear Thomas", "Dear Ms Rivers". Find the name — it's usually on the studio site, the advert or LinkedIn. "Dear Hiring Manager" is acceptable if there's genuinely no name. "Dear Sir/Madam" and "To whom it may concern" read as though you didn't look.
  2. Open with the role and the reason — together Not "I am writing to apply for…" — they know, it's in the subject line. Say what you're applying for and why them in the same sentence: something specific about their game, their art, their team.
  3. Prove it, once One or two pieces of evidence that match what they asked for. A named project, a tool, a number. This is not your CV again — pick the one thing that best answers their advert and explain why it's relevant.
  4. Show you've done your homework One line that could only be written about this studio. A game they made, a talk someone gave, a decision you noticed. This is the line that proves the letter wasn't sent to forty places.
  5. Close by making the next step easy When you're available, what you can send, how to reach you. "I hope to hear from you" leaves them to do the work. "I can send a playable build and I'm free any weekday after 3pm" doesn't.

Length

One page. Roughly 250–400 words. If yours is longer, the reader is doing work you should have done. If it's under 150, there probably isn't enough evidence in it.

Format

PDF, matching the look of your CV — same typeface, same accent colour, same header. It's part of your identity, so it should look like it came from the same person.

The test before you send it

Swap the studio name for a different studio. If the letter still makes sense, it is generic — and generic letters are the ones people stop reading after two lines. Every good cover letter breaks when you change the name.

04 — Openings

Where most of them die

Readers decide whether to keep going within the first couple of lines. Six real weak sentences, each with a stronger rewrite. Pick the one that would make you keep reading — and read the explanation either way, because that's where the skill is.

06 — The real one

A real letter, to a real studio

Why this one first

It's short

A playtester letter is a few paragraphs. It's the right size for a first real cover letter — long enough to get the structure right, short enough to get it right in a lesson.

Someone reads it

Most first letters go into the void. This one goes to a named person who will actually read it and decide. That changes how carefully you write.

It's practice for the next one

Get the shape right here, on something low-stakes, before you write to an external studio where you only get one go.

What a strong playtester letter shows

  • What kind of player you are The genres you play, and specifically anything close to this one — squad games, strategy, games with formations or unit control. Be honest; a good playtester isn't the best player, it's the most observant one.
  • That you can explain what you notice Playtesting is only useful if you can say what happened and why it felt wrong. If you've written bug reports, given feedback in a jam, or written clear notes in your reflective log, say so.
  • That you'll actually turn up When you're available, and that you understand feedback has to be specific to be any use.
